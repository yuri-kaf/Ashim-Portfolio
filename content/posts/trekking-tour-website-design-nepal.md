Think about who is on the other end of a trekking company's website. Someone in another time zone, on their phone, with a dozen tabs open. Every tab is a Kathmandu company offering the same route at roughly the same price, and half of them use a photo of the same mountain at the same sunrise. They've never been to Nepal. They're about to send money to a business they found an hour ago, in a country where they can't walk into the office if something goes wrong.

The website is the only evidence they have. The complaint I hear most from trekking and tour operators is that the site gets visitors but not bookings. Looked at from the traveller's chair, the reason is usually that the site answers the questions the owner thinks about, not the ones the traveller is asking.

This is a teardown of those questions, roughly in the order a foreign traveller asks them. I'm not naming any companies. The patterns are common enough that you'll recognise them.

## Trust comes before the itinerary

Before anyone reads day three of your route, they're deciding whether you're real. Most of that decision happens in the header, the footer and the About page.

**Show your registration as text.** Put your Department of Tourism trekking licence number and your company registration number in the footer and on the About page. If you're a member of the [Trekking Agencies' Association of Nepal](https://www.taan.org.np/page/to-become-a-member), which asks for that licence before it accepts a member, show the membership number as well. A strip of association logos is decoration. A number is something a careful traveller can check.

**Use photos of your actual team.** Guides with their names, the office, a group on a trip you actually ran. Stock photos of smiling hikers make you look like a reseller, and travellers who have been burned before know the difference.

**Link reviews to where they live.** A carousel of five-star quotes signed "Sarah, UK" proves nothing, because you wrote the page it sits on. Link out to your profiles on TripAdvisor, Google or wherever your reviews actually are, and let people read the less glowing ones too. A company with a few honest four-star reviews reads as more real than one with a wall of perfect ones.

**Give a real address and a real person.** A street address with a map, an office phone number, and the name of whoever answers enquiries. "Contact our team" is weaker than a name and a face.

## The itinerary page does most of the selling

Once they trust you enough to keep reading, the itinerary page is where they decide. It has to answer the things a first-time visitor to the Himalaya is nervous about, and most of that comes down to altitude and money.

The most common problem I see is the itinerary as a wall of prose: "Day 5: After breakfast we trek through beautiful rhododendron forests…" It's pleasant to read and tells the traveller almost nothing they need. They want the walking hours, and the altitude they'll sleep at each night.

Here's what a complete trekking itinerary page covers:

| Element | What to show | What the traveller is really asking |
|---|---|---|
| Trip summary, top of the page | Duration, maximum altitude, difficulty, group size, start and end point, price from | Is this the right trip for me? |
| Day-by-day plan | Route, walking hours, altitude of that night's stop, where you sleep | What will each day feel like? |
| Altitude profile | Sleeping altitudes in a simple chart or table, acclimatisation days marked, metres and feet | Is this safe for my body? |
| Difficulty | A plain description of the fitness and terrain involved, not a single word like "moderate" | Can I actually do this? |
| Price | USD per person, how it changes with group size, single supplement | What will I pay? |
| Included and excluded | Two lists side by side: permits, guide, porter, meals, flights, transfers, tips, insurance | Which costs will surprise me later? |
| Departure dates | Fixed group departures, or "private trip, any date you choose" | Can I go when I want? |
| Permits | Which ones the route needs, who arranges them, whether they're in the price | Do I have to sort anything myself? |
| Accommodation | Lodge, teahouse or hotel for each night, and what "basic" means there | Where will I sleep? |
| Safety and rescue | Guide first-aid training, the plan for altitude sickness, the insurance you require | What if something goes wrong? |
| Packing list | What to bring, what can be rented or bought in Kathmandu | What do I need to buy before I fly? |
| Next step | WhatsApp button, enquiry form, deposit option | How do I start? |

Put the summary block at the top, where it's visible on a phone without scrolling. The rest can sit further down in collapsible sections, as long as the day-by-day plan is real text on the page and not a PDF download. A PDF is a slow download on mobile data and awkward to read on a small screen, and a traveller pinching and zooming through one is a traveller about to open the next tab.

## Price in dollars, and say what the dollars cover

Show the price in US dollars, the currency international trekking trips are usually compared in. "Price on request" is the quickest way to lose someone who is comparing. They won't write in to ask. They'll go to the next tab, where a competitor has put a number.

A useful price section shows:

- a "from" price per person, and how it changes with group size
- whether that price is for a group departure or a private trip
- the single supplement, if there is one
- what changes by season, if anything does
- the deposit amount and when the balance is due

Then put the included and excluded lists right next to the price, not in the booking terms. Travellers don't mind that tips and international flights aren't included. They mind finding out after they've paid.

## Group departures and private trips

Plenty of travellers are choosing between joining a group and going private, and they want both options on the same page with the price difference spelled out. If you run fixed departures, list the dates and the places left, and keep that list current. A departure calendar still showing last spring's dates tells the traveller nobody is minding the website, and they will wonder who is minding the trips.

## Permits, and when you last checked

Travellers don't expect to understand Nepal's permit system. They want to know which permits their route needs, who arranges them, and whether the cost is already in the price.

Permit and guide rules in Nepal do change, so date that section: "Permit information last checked: October 2026." An itinerary page that states last season's rule with confidence does more damage than one that says the rules change and you'll confirm the current ones when they book. A date shows that someone is paying attention.

## Safety, insurance and rescue

Owners tend to leave this section thin because it feels negative. Travellers read it more closely than anything else on the page, especially for high-altitude routes.

Say what training your guides have, what happens if someone shows signs of altitude sickness, and how an evacuation gets arranged. If you require travel insurance that covers helicopter rescue at altitude, say so on the trip page, not only in the terms and conditions. A traveller who discovers that requirement a week before departure is an unhappy traveller, and unhappy travellers write reviews.

## Fast pages on a traveller's phone

Most of the research happens on phones, in whatever spare minutes the traveller has. Some of it happens after they land, on a local SIM, and even as 4G spreads, [users in Nepal still face slow speeds and patchy coverage outside the cities](https://kathmandupost.com/science-technology/2026/04/14/4g-keeps-growing-fast-in-nepal-while-users-still-face-slow-speeds-and-patchy-coverage).

Trekking sites have a particular weakness here, which is enormous mountain photographs. A full-width hero image straight off a camera can weigh more than the rest of the page combined. Resize and compress every image, serve modern formats, don't autoplay a background video, and test the site on a mid-range Android phone over mobile data rather than on the office Wi-Fi. If the itinerary takes too long to appear, the traveller never finds out how good the trip is.

## A clear path to enquire

WhatsApp is where a lot of these conversations happen, and travellers are used to it. Put a WhatsApp button on every trip page, and have it open a chat with a pre-filled message that includes the trip name, so your team doesn't have to start with "which trek?"

Just don't make it the only way in. In September 2025 Nepal [blocked 26 unregistered platforms, WhatsApp among them](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2025/09/04/nepal-bans-facebook-and-other-major-social-media-platforms-over-non-compliance), and the block lasted several days. An enquiry form on your own website doesn't depend on any one platform staying available.

Keep the form short: trip, rough dates, group size, name, email, and an optional WhatsApp number. Send an automatic reply confirming it arrived and saying when a person will answer. Travellers in a different time zone are far more patient when they know the wait is normal.

## Taking a deposit by international card

This is where a lot of trekking sites fall back on "we'll email you our bank details," and where a lot of bookings quietly die. Asking a stranger abroad to make an international bank transfer to a company they found online is a big ask.

eSewa, Khalti, Fonepay and ConnectIPS are how Nepali customers pay, and they're worth having for domestic trips. I've covered how they differ in [a separate post on website payments](/blog/esewa-khalti-fonepay-connectips-website-payments). A traveller from abroad almost certainly doesn't have a Nepali wallet or bank account, though, so for them you need a card gateway from a Nepali bank.

These exist. Himalayan Bank's [e-commerce gateway](https://himalayanbank.com/en/card-service/e-commerce) takes Visa, Mastercard, American Express and UnionPay. Nabil Bank's gateway [accepts Visa, Mastercard and UnionPay cards issued in Nepal or abroad](https://thehimalayantimes.com/business/nabils-electronic-payment-gateway). Charges and paperwork vary by bank and are quoted when you apply, so talk to your bank before the site promises card payments.

Once the gateway is connected, the design work is in the steps around it:

- Show the deposit amount, the balance and its due date before the payment page, not on it.
- Say which currency the card will be charged in.
- Warn them that their own bank may ask them to confirm the payment with a code or an app, so they don't think the page has frozen.
- Put the cancellation and refund policy next to the pay button, in plain words.
- Email a receipt straight away, with the trip, the dates, the amount paid and a named contact.

A deposit taken this way feels like booking with a company. A transfer to an account number pasted into an email feels like a risk.

## Languages, units and dates

If a real share of your enquiries comes from one language, translate your most-booked trip pages properly, and have someone who speaks the language check them. A translate widget in the corner produces the kind of text that makes a careful traveller more nervous, not less. Give each language its own URLs and connect them with hreflang tags, so a traveller searching in German lands on the German page.

Two smaller details cost nothing. Show altitudes in both metres and feet, because American travellers think in feet. And write dates as "12 October 2026" rather than "12/10/2026", which a traveller in the US will read as the 10th of December.

## Looking like yourself

Scroll through enough trekking sites and they blur together: the same blues, the same sunrise, the same welcome paragraph. When everyone looks alike, travellers fall back on price. A clear [brand identity](/services/branding), and your own photographs of your own guides on your own trips, give them another reason to pick you. Most of the difference in a well-designed [trekking website](/services/web-design) comes from that: less decoration, more evidence.

## Questions trekking companies ask about their websites

### What should a trekking company website include?

At minimum, your licence and registration numbers, photos of your real team, links to independent reviews, full itinerary pages with daily altitudes and walking times, prices in USD with inclusions and exclusions, departure dates and a clear way to enquire. A way to pay a deposit by card is the next thing to add.

### Can a trekking company in Nepal accept international card payments on its website?

Yes, through a Nepali bank's payment gateway. Himalayan Bank and Nabil Bank both run gateways that accept international cards. The requirements and charges vary, and the bank quotes them when you apply.

### Should a trekking website show its prices?

Yes. Show a "from" price per person in US dollars, with what it includes and how it changes with group size. Travellers comparing several companies rarely write in to ask for a price they can already see somewhere else.

### Do I need a separate website for each language?

No. Keep one domain with a section for each language, and connect the versions with hreflang tags. Start with the trips that get the most enquiries rather than translating the whole site at once.
