Most of the website disputes I hear about in Kathmandu don't start with bad design. They start with a quotation that was one line long: "Website design," and a number. Nobody wrote down how many pages there would be, who writes the text, whose name the domain goes in, or what happens when something breaks six months after launch. Both sides filled the gaps with their own assumptions, and the assumptions didn't match.

This post is for the business owner who has two or three names on the table, freelancer or studio, and wants to vet them before paying an advance. Choosing between an agency, a freelancer and an in-house hire is a separate decision, and [Limi Creatives has written that one up](https://limicreatives.com/blogs/agency-freelancer-or-in-house). This is about what to ask once you're already talking to someone.

A disclosure: I design websites for a living, so I'm on the other side of these conversations most weeks. Every question below works just as well on me.

## Portfolio and process

**1. Can you walk me through one project, from brief to launch?**

Not the whole portfolio. One project. You want to hear what the client needed, what the designer suggested, what changed along the way and why. A good answer has specifics in it, something like "their customers kept calling to ask about delivery areas, so we put the delivery map on the home page." A weak answer is a scroll through pretty screens with no reasoning behind any of them.

**2. Which of these are live, and can I speak to one of those clients?**

Mockups are easy to make look good. A live site shows whether the design survived real content, real photos and a developer. Asking for a reference is normal, and anyone with a few years of work behind them should have a past client or two willing to take a five-minute call.

**3. Who will actually do the work?**

Studios often pitch with their most senior person and hand the project to someone junior. Freelancers sometimes design the site and pass the development to a friend. Neither is automatically wrong, but you should know. A good answer names the people, says who your day-to-day contact is, and tells you who picks up if that person is away.

**4. What happens between signing and seeing the first design?**

If the answer is "we'll send the home page in three days," they haven't asked you anything yet. A good process starts with questions about your customers, then a sitemap, then wireframes that settle the structure before anyone argues about colour. You should know when you'll be asked for feedback and what exactly you're approving each time.

**5. How will you make sure it works on phones?**

Nepal has more mobile connections than people, 32.4 million of them according to [DataReportal's Digital 2026 report](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-nepal). For most businesses here, the phone version is the website. A good answer: they design the mobile layout first or alongside desktop, and they test on real mid-range Android phones over mobile data, rather than by shrinking a browser window on a laptop.

## Scope and ownership

**6. What exactly is included, page by page?**

Ask for it in writing. The page list, the features (contact form, map, WhatsApp button, booking, payments) and anything that is specifically not included. A good answer is a document. A message saying "full website, everything included" isn't scope. It's a future argument.

**7. Who writes the content and supplies the photos?**

This is where most timelines slip. The design is approved and the site sits empty for a month waiting for the "About us" text. A good answer is clear about who owns each piece. If the designer is writing copy or arranging photography, it's priced as its own line. If it's on you, there's a date it's due.

**8. How many revision rounds, and what counts as a revision?**

Revisions need a limit and a definition. A good answer sounds like: "Two rounds on the wireframes, two on the visual design, and changes to the agreed structure after sign-off are quoted separately." That protects you as much as the designer, because it forces decisions to happen at the stage where they're cheap to make.

**9. Whose name will the domain and hosting be in?**

Yours, every time. For a .np domain, [register.com.np](https://register.com.np/how-to-register-np-domain) lets an organisation register under its company, product, service or brand name, and the registry sends renewal reminders 15 days before expiry. Make sure that reminder lands in your inbox, not in the inbox of a freelancer who has since moved abroad. A good answer: "It'll be registered to your business with your email address. I'll just need access to set it up."

**10. What will it be built on, and why that?**

A static site, WordPress or something custom each suit a different kind of business, and I've covered the trade-offs in [static, WordPress or custom](/blog/static-wordpress-or-custom-website-nepal). The good answer here is a reason tied to you: how often you'll update the site and who will do the updating. "We always use X" is a reason tied to them. While you're on the subject, ask who owns the licence for any paid theme or plugin.

**11. How is payment split, and what happens if we stop halfway?**

Paying an advance is normal. Paying everything up front isn't. A good answer ties each payment to something you can see: an approved design, a working site on a test link, the launch. It also says what you walk away with if the project stops midway, such as the designs finished so far, so neither side is held hostage.

## After launch

**12. What do I get at handover, and will you show me how to use it?**

Ask for the list before you start: the Figma or design files, the source code or theme files, the admin logins, the hosting control panel login, and a note of every paid plugin or service the site depends on. For what it's worth, a build-ready Figma file is part of what I hand over on [web design projects](/services/web-design). A good answer also includes a short training session, or a screen recording you can rewatch, covering the pages you'll actually edit.

**13. If something breaks after launch, what happens?**

Things break. A form stops sending, or a layout falls apart on one phone model. A good answer defines a bug-fix window after launch, separates a bug (something that doesn't work as agreed) from a new request (something you've thought of since), and says how quickly they'll respond.

**14. What does maintenance cost, and is it optional?**

Updates, backups and security checks are real work, especially on WordPress. A good answer prices maintenance as a separate, optional line, monthly or yearly, and lists what it covers. You should be able to stop paying for it and move your site elsewhere without losing anything.

**15. What will the site do for search on day one?**

A good designer sets up the basics: a sensible title and description on every page, clean headings, images that load quickly, a sitemap, and Google Search Console connected to your own account. What they shouldn't do is promise page one of Google. Ranking takes months of work after launch, and [Limi Creatives explains why](https://limicreatives.com/blogs/how-long-does-seo-actually-take-in-nepal). Anyone who guarantees a position is either guessing or selling you something else.

## Red flags in a website quotation

Most quotes I'm asked to look over have at least one of these. One on its own isn't always fatal. Three together usually are.

- **No written scope.** If the pages and features aren't listed, you and the designer are agreeing to two different websites.
- **"Unlimited revisions."** It sounds generous. In practice it means nobody has planned when decisions get made, so the project drifts until the revisions quietly stop being unlimited.
- **No mention of mobile.** If the quote doesn't say the site will be designed and tested for phones, assume it won't be.
- **The domain registered in the designer's name.** Convenient at the start, and a real problem the day you want to change designers.
- **Hosting you can't access.** If only the designer can log in, only the designer can fix, move or renew your site.
- **No handover of Figma, source files or admin logins.** You're paying for a website, not renting one.
- **A price with no breakdown.** A single number tells you nothing about where the effort goes, and makes it impossible to compare two quotes fairly.
- **A template sold as custom.** Templates aren't bad, and on a tight budget they can be the right call. Paying a custom price for one is the problem. Ask which theme or template the design starts from. An honest designer will simply tell you.

## What a well-structured quote looks like

You don't need a twenty-page proposal. You need line items you can question. Something like this, with the prices filled in:

| Line item | What it should spell out | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery and sitemap | Meetings included, agreed page list | — |
| Wireframes | Pages covered, feedback rounds | — |
| Visual design | Pages designed for mobile and desktop, revision rounds | — |
| Development | Platform, pages built, forms and integrations | — |
| Content | Who writes the text, who supplies photos, for how many pages | — |
| Domain and hosting | Registered in your name, yearly renewal shown separately | — |
| Basic SEO setup | Page titles and descriptions, sitemap, Search Console | — |
| Handover and training | Files, logins, training session | — |
| Post-launch support | Bug-fix window and what it covers | — |
| Maintenance (optional) | Monthly or yearly, what's included | — |

Under the table you want the payment schedule, a timeline that includes the dates you're responsible for, and a short list of what's excluded. If two quotes both have that, you can compare them line by line instead of total against total.

## What to prepare before you ask for a quote

Designers quote better when you give them something to quote on. Before you contact anyone, write down:

- **The goal.** What should a visitor do: call, message on WhatsApp, book, buy, send an enquiry? Pick the one that matters most.
- **Three sites you like, and why.** Be specific. "Their menu is easy to read on a phone" is useful. "It looks modern" isn't. They don't have to be Nepali or in your industry.
- **The pages you think you need.** A rough list is fine. A good designer will push back on some of it.
- **Who owns the content.** Who writes the text, who supplies the photos, and who has the final say.
- **Your deadline, and what's behind it.** A launch, a trade fair, the festival season. A real reason lets the designer plan backwards from it.
- **A budget range.** It feels like giving away your negotiating position. It isn't. It tells the designer whether to propose the full version or the version that fits, and saves everyone a week of back and forth.

That list doubles as a basic website requirements checklist. Send the same one to everyone you're talking to, and the quotes that come back will actually be comparable.

## Questions people ask about hiring a web designer in Nepal

### How much does it cost to hire a web designer in Nepal?

It depends on the number of pages, the features, and how much of the content the designer handles. As a typical range, a small business website of 5–10 pages sits around NPR 25,000 – 50,000, and I've broken the ranges down in [what UI/UX actually costs in Nepal](/blog/ui-ux-in-nepal-what-it-actually-costs). Domain, hosting and maintenance are usually separate, recurring costs.

### Is it normal to pay an advance to a web designer?

Yes. Paying part of the fee up front is standard for freelancers and studios alike. What isn't normal is paying the full amount before you've seen any work. Tie the remaining payments to milestones you can check for yourself.

### Is a freelance web designer or a website design company in Nepal the safer choice?

Neither is automatically safer. There are careful freelancers and careless studios, and the other way round. The fifteen questions above work on both. If you're still weighing the two, [Limi Creatives' breakdown](https://limicreatives.com/blogs/agency-freelancer-or-in-house) covers the trade-offs.

### What should go in a website requirements checklist?

Your main goal for the site, a rough page list, the features you need, who is responsible for content, three reference sites with your reasons, a deadline and a budget range. It doesn't need to be long. One page you send to every designer you speak to is enough.
