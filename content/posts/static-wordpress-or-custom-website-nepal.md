One of the first questions on almost every website call I take in Kathmandu is "How much for a dynamic website?" I understand why people ask it that way. Agencies here have sold "static" and "dynamic" packages for years, and since the dynamic one always costs more, it must be the better one.

The trouble is that "dynamic" doesn't tell me what the site needs to do. A restaurant that changes its menu twice a year and a trekking company taking deposits from abroad both ask for a dynamic website. They need very different things.

This post is a decision guide, not a price list. It explains the kinds of website you'll be offered, which businesses each one suits, and what each costs to keep alive after launch.

## What static and dynamic actually mean

A static website is a set of finished pages. When someone opens it, the server hands over a page that already exists. Nothing is looked up or assembled on the spot.

A dynamic website builds pages when someone asks for them, usually by pulling content from a database. That's what lets you log into an admin panel, change a price and see it on the site without calling a developer.

When most agencies in Nepal say "dynamic", that admin panel is what they mean. It's a fair thing to want. But you can get it in more than one way, and the way matters more than the label.

## The four kinds of website you'll be offered

### A static site

Pages are built once and served as they are. Hosting is cheap and the site is very fast. Speed matters more here than people think: mobile data is still [slow and patchy outside the cities](https://kathmandupost.com/science-technology/2026/04/14/4g-keeps-growing-fast-in-nepal-while-users-still-face-slow-speeds-and-patchy-coverage), and a light page loads on a weak connection where a heavy one gives up. With no login or database on the live site, there's also very little for anyone to break into.

The catch is editing. Unless the site is connected to some kind of content editor, every change goes through a developer. For a site that changes twice a year, that's fine. For one that changes every week, it gets old quickly.

### A WordPress site

WordPress is a content management system, or CMS. The pages live in a database and you edit them from a dashboard. It's the most common version of what people here call a dynamic site, and developers who know it are easy to find in Kathmandu.

Its strength is plugins. Forms, bookings, SEO settings, an online shop through WooCommerce, even local payments (Khalti publishes a [WooCommerce plugin](https://github.com/khalti/khalti-woocommerce)). Its weakness is the same thing. Every plugin is code that someone has to keep updated, and an out-of-date plugin is one of the most common ways small WordPress sites get broken into.

### A website builder

Wix, Squarespace, Shopify and similar services give you templates, hosting and an editor under one subscription. You don't deal with servers or updates, and a reasonably organised owner can run one without a developer.

You rent rather than own, though. The subscription never ends, you're limited to what the platform supports, and moving away later usually means rebuilding. Check payments before you commit. These platforms are built mainly around international card payments, and support for eSewa, Khalti or Fonepay varies. If your customers pay by wallet or QR, confirm that works on the plan you're buying.

### A custom-built site or web app

Designed and coded for your business specifically, often with its own admin panel, user accounts, dashboards or integrations with other systems. This is what you need when the website is the product: a booking platform, a marketplace, a SaaS tool, anything where users log in and do something.

It's also the most expensive to build and the most dependent on the team that built it. If that developer disappears, so does most of your ability to change it.

## Which kind suits which business in Nepal

These are starting points, not rules. The details of your business will move them.

**A clinic** mostly needs to be found and trusted: doctors, timings, services, location, and a phone number that works. A static site or a small WordPress site does this well. Online booking is worth adding only if someone at the front desk will actually manage the bookings.

**A restaurant** changes its menu and opening hours, and that's about it. Most people find a restaurant through Google Maps or social media before they ever see its website, which is why the team at Limi Creatives wrote a separate guide on [local SEO for restaurants and retail](https://limicreatives.com/blogs/local-seo-for-restaurants-and-retail). A fast static site or a builder site with a clear, readable menu is usually enough.

**A trekking or tour company** publishes itineraries, updates prices and departure dates every season, and usually takes deposits from foreign customers. WordPress fits well here, because the content changes often and there are plugins for itineraries and enquiry forms. Pair it with a bank card gateway for international payments. I've covered those in the post on [eSewa, Khalti, Fonepay and ConnectIPS](/blog/esewa-khalti-fonepay-connectips-website-payments).

**An online shop** needs products, stock, a checkout and local payments. WooCommerce on WordPress or a builder like Shopify can both work, provided the payment methods your customers use are supported. A custom build only makes sense when your ordering is unusual: made-to-order products, wholesale pricing, delivery rules nobody else has.

**A SaaS or app startup** needs two things that often get mixed up: a marketing site and the product. The product is custom. The marketing site shouldn't be. Put it on a static site or a builder so the founders can change the homepage without waiting for the next developer sprint.

## A decision matrix

| Business | How often content changes | Needs payments or bookings? | Usually the right fit | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clinic | A few times a year | Sometimes, for appointments | Static site or small WordPress site | Booking tools nobody at reception checks |
| Restaurant | Menu and hours, occasionally | Rarely | Static site or website builder | The menu uploaded as a blurry photo |
| Trekking or tour company | Every season | Deposits, often from abroad | WordPress with a bank card gateway | Plugin sprawl and pages slowed by huge photos |
| Online shop | Weekly or daily | Yes, local wallets and cash on delivery | WooCommerce or a builder with local payments | A builder that can't take the payments your customers use |
| SaaS or app startup | Marketing site often, product constantly | Subscriptions or in-app | Custom app plus a static or builder marketing site | Building the marketing site inside the app |
| Consultant or portfolio | Rarely | No | Static site or website builder | Paying for features you'll never use |

If your business sits between two rows, pick the simpler option. It's easier to move up from a simple site later than to keep paying to maintain a complicated one you didn't need.

## What a website costs after launch

Price is the first question, and most price comparisons stop at the build. I've published typical ranges in my post on [what UI/UX costs in Nepal](/blog/ui-ux-in-nepal-what-it-actually-costs): a small business website of 5 to 10 pages typically falls around NPR 25,000 to 50,000, and multi-flow platforms and marketplaces start around NPR 100,000. Those are typical ranges, not quotes.

Search for dynamic website prices in Nepal and you'll find plenty of lower numbers, and some of them are fine. What they rarely show is the running cost. Every site carries some of these:

- **Hosting renewal.** Usually yearly. Cheap first-year offers often renew at a higher rate, so read the renewal price, not the headline one.
- **Domain renewal.** Also yearly, and the reminder goes to whoever registered it, which may not be you.
- **Plugin and software updates.** Mostly a WordPress cost. Someone has to run them and check nothing broke afterwards.
- **Security and backups.** Who notices if the site is hacked, and how old is the last backup?
- **Builder subscriptions.** A Wix or Shopify-style site charges for as long as it exists, plus any paid apps you add.
- **Whoever edits the content.** This is the cost people forget. If only the agency can make changes, every edit becomes a small invoice and a wait.

Roughly, a static site is cheap on most of these lines. WordPress is cheap to host but needs regular maintenance. A builder bundles most of it into the subscription. A custom build needs a developer on call for as long as it runs.

## A note on .com.np domains

A lot of Nepali businesses want a .com.np address, and it's a sensible signal for local customers. The rules are worth knowing before you settle on a name, because the registry is strict about them.

According to [register.com.np](https://register.com.np/how-to-register-np-domain), a personal .np domain has to be based only on the applicant's own name. An organisation can register its company name, or the name of a product, service or brand, backed by documents such as a company or firm registration certificate, a PAN registration certificate or a trademark filing. So you can't register a catchy service name as an individual and expect it to pass.

A .com.np domain is free for eligible applicants, but it isn't set-and-forget. The [registry says](https://register.com.np/) it emails the registered domain administrator 15 days before expiry, and it has been reverifying domains since December 2024. If an agency or a former employee registered your domain with their email address, you won't see that reminder. Make sure the registered contact is someone inside your business.

## The one question to ask any agency

Before you compare prices, ask this: **"Who can edit this after you hand it over, and how?"**

A good answer names a person on your team and the tool they'll use. It says what they can change themselves, like text, photos, prices and blog posts, and what will still need a developer, like layout, new page types and features. It might include a short handover session or a written guide.

A worrying answer is vague. "Just call us" is fine for a site that changes once a year. For one that changes every week, it's expensive.

While you're at it, ask who will own the domain, the hosting account and the admin logins, and make sure the answer is you. ## Questions people ask about website types in Nepal

### What's the difference between a static and a dynamic website?

A static site serves pages that are already built. A dynamic site builds pages from a database when someone visits, which is what lets you edit content from an admin panel. Dynamic isn't automatically better. It's more flexible, and it needs more upkeep.

### Is a static website enough for a small business?

Often, yes. If your content changes a few times a year and you don't take payments online, a static site is fast and cheap to host, with little to go wrong. The trade-off is that edits usually go through a developer.

### How much does a dynamic website cost in Nepal?

It depends on what "dynamic" means for your project, which is why I'd rather scope than quote. The typical ranges I've published put a 5 to 10 page small business website at around NPR 25,000 to 50,000, with multi-flow platforms starting around NPR 100,000, before hosting, domain and maintenance. The [UI/UX cost post](/blog/ui-ux-in-nepal-what-it-actually-costs) explains what pushes a project up or down.

### Is WordPress safe for a business website?

It's as safe as its upkeep. Most problems come from outdated plugins and themes, weak admin passwords and nobody checking. Keep the plugin list short, update it regularly, and make one person responsible for backups.

### Can I get a .com.np domain for my business?

Yes, if the name matches your company, product, service or brand and you have supporting documents such as a registration certificate, PAN certificate or trademark filing. Personal .np domains must be based on the applicant's own name.
