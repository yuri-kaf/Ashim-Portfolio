"We get all our orders from Facebook. Why would we need a website?"

I hear some version of that in almost every first meeting with a business in Kathmandu, and it is a fair question. For a lot of shops it is even the right instinct. Facebook is where the customers already are: [DataReportal's 2026 numbers for Nepal](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-nepal) put Facebook's ad reach at 14.8 million people, in a country of about 29.6 million. Nobody has to learn anything new to message you there.

So this isn't a post telling you to abandon your page. It is about the point where a page stops being enough, how to recognise it, and what a website has to do for a small business here. Most of what gets sold as a "business website" in Nepal is more than a small business needs.

## When a Facebook page really is enough

If most of these are true, you can put the website money into better photos, better products or better ads for now:

- Customers already know you, or find you through people who do.
- You sell a small range with prices that rarely change.
- Almost every order happens in Messenger, WhatsApp or Viber and ends in cash on delivery.
- Nobody has ever asked you for a website, a company profile or a catalogue.
- You serve one area, and people find you by walking past or by word of mouth.

A momo stall with a queue doesn't need a website. Neither does a home baker who is already fully booked for Tihar. If that's you, the rest of this post is for later.

## Where a page stops being enough

The signs that a page has become a ceiling are fairly consistent.

**You answer the same five questions all day.** What's the price, do you deliver to Bhaktapur, do you take eSewa, what are the sizes, where exactly are you. Each one is a customer who wanted to decide and couldn't without asking. A page that answers them before anyone has to type is the cheapest salesperson you will ever hire.

**People search for what you sell, and you are not there.** Social media is where people discover things they weren't looking for. Google is where they go once they know what they want: "cake delivery Lalitpur", "dental clinic near Baneshwor", "trekking agency in Thamel". A Facebook page rarely shows up for searches like that, and when it does you have almost no control over what it says.

**You sell to people who check before they buy.** Businesses asking for a quotation, NGOs, schools, anyone in the diaspora, tourists planning from abroad. These buyers want a proper address on the web and a clear list of what you do, and some of them will ask for the link before they ask for a price. A Facebook page can still look like a side hustle to them, even when it isn't one.

**You are paying for ads.** Boosting a post sends people to a feed where your offer sits between a friend's wedding photos and a meme. Sending them to one page that exists only to explain the offer and take the enquiry almost always converts better. [What a healthy ad budget looks like](https://limicreatives.com/blogs/what-a-healthy-ad-budget-looks-like-in-nepal) is a separate question. Where the ad sends people is a design question, and it is usually the cheaper one to fix.

**Your prices, menu or catalogue change often.** Facebook albums and pinned posts are a poor catalogue. They get buried, they can't be searched, and old prices keep circulating in screenshots long after you've changed them.

## The week Facebook went dark

On 4 September 2025, Nepal blocked 26 social media platforms that had not registered with the government, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and YouTube among them. [The block was lifted on 9 September](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2025/09/04/nepal-bans-facebook-and-other-major-social-media-platforms-over-non-compliance). TikTok and Viber had registered, so they stayed up. The same thing had happened with TikTok a couple of years earlier: [it was banned in November 2023 and not allowed back until August 2024](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2024/08/23/nepal-lifts-its-ban-on-tiktok).

Whatever you think about the reasons, the lesson for a business is simple. A page on someone else's platform is rented space. The platform can change its reach, its rules or its availability, and your customers go wherever it sends them. For five days in September, a shop that existed only as a Facebook page and a WhatsApp number didn't exist at all.

A website is the one address you own. It doesn't replace your page. It is where the page, the ads, the Google listing and the printed flyer all point, and it stays put whatever happens to any one of them.

## What people check before they buy

When someone hears about you on TikTok or sees your post shared in a group, the next thing they do is usually a quick check. They search your name. They look for an address, a phone number, some sign that you have been around for more than a month, and a price.

If that search finds a tidy page with the answers, you have passed the check. If it finds nothing, or only a Facebook page with the last post from three weeks ago, some people will still message you. Some of them won't.

This matters more the more expensive the thing you sell. Nobody researches a plate of chowmein. Almost everybody researches a clinic, a school, a contractor, a trek, an electronics shop or a wedding photographer.

## What a website needs to be for a small business

Here is where a lot of businesses get oversold. For most small businesses in Nepal a good first website is one well-built page, or a handful of pages, that does a few things properly:

| Section | What it answers | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| What you sell, in one sentence | "Am I in the right place?" | People decide in seconds, most of them on a phone |
| Prices or price ranges | "Can I afford this?" | Stops the "price?" DMs, and filters out people who were never going to buy |
| Delivery areas and time | "Will it reach me?" | The most common question for anyone selling across the valley |
| How to pay | "Can I use eSewa, Khalti, a bank transfer or cash?" | Payment worry kills orders quietly |
| Phone, WhatsApp and Viber buttons | "How do I ask?" | One tap, not a number to copy |
| Location map and opening hours | "Where are you and are you open?" | Makes walk-ins and pickups easy |
| Real photos and a few reviews | "Are they real?" | The trust check above |
| Five FAQs | Everything else you answer every day | Your DMs get shorter |

It has to be fast on a phone. [Nepal Telecommunications Authority figures reported this April](https://kathmandupost.com/science-technology/2026/04/14/4g-keeps-growing-fast-in-nepal-while-users-still-face-slow-speeds-and-patchy-coverage) show 4G is by far the main way people are online, and the same report notes slow speeds and patchy coverage outside the cities. A site built from huge images and sliders that loads beautifully on office Wi-Fi can take ages on mobile data in Pokhara. Design for the phone first, then check it still looks right on a laptop.

What you usually don't need on day one:

- a full online shop with a cart and accounts, unless people already want to order without messaging you;
- a blog you won't have time to write;
- ten pages that repeat the same three paragraphs;
- an "About us" story longer than your product list.

## Use them together

The businesses that do this well don't pick one. They give each channel its own job.

**Social media is for discovery.** Reels, posts and stories are how new people find you and how existing customers remember you. Keep doing that, and keep it human.

**The website is for the decision.** Put the link in your bio, in every ad and on every product post. When someone is interested, send them to the one place that answers everything and makes the next step obvious.

**Messages are for closing.** WhatsApp, Viber and Messenger are still where most sales here actually happen. The website's job is to make those conversations shorter and warmer. Someone who has already seen your prices and delivery areas messages to buy, not to ask.

If you want to be found on Google Maps as well, that is mostly about your Google Business Profile, not your site. Limi Creatives has a [plain-language guide to local SEO](https://limicreatives.com/blogs/local-seo-for-restaurants-and-retail) that covers it.

## What it costs

Less than most people fear, if you keep the scope honest. The typical ranges I see for design work in Nepal are in my post on [what UI/UX design actually costs](/blog/ui-ux-in-nepal-what-it-actually-costs):

| Project | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Simple landing page or single-flow design | NPR 10,000 – 25,000 |
| Small business website (5–10 pages) | NPR 25,000 – 50,000 |

On top of that you pay for hosting, which renews every year, and for someone to update the site when prices change. Ask about that second cost before you sign anything. A website nobody can edit goes out of date as fast as a pinned Facebook post. A .com.np domain is free for eligible businesses through the [.np registry](https://register.com.np/how-to-register-np-domain), and for a business serving Nepali customers it signals "local" in a way a .com doesn't.

## A five-question test

Answer honestly:

1. Do you answer the same questions in your DMs every single day?
2. Do you sell to businesses, institutions, tourists or the diaspora?
3. Are you spending money on ads or boosts?
4. Do people search Google for the thing you sell in your area?
5. Would you lose real money if Facebook went down for a week again?

If you answered yes to two or more, a simple website will pay for itself. If you answered yes to none, keep your page, post good photos and come back to this when business grows.

## Questions people ask me

### Is a Facebook page enough for a small business in Nepal?

For some, yes: a small range, local customers, orders by message and cash on delivery. It stops being enough once you run ads, sell to people who research first, or spend your day answering the same questions. The platform block in September 2025 is also a good reason not to depend on a single app.

### Will my website show up on Google automatically?

Not automatically, and not overnight. A well-built site with clear page titles, your location and real content gives Google something to work with, but ranking takes time and some ongoing work. [How long SEO actually takes in Nepal](https://limicreatives.com/blogs/how-long-does-seo-actually-take-in-nepal) is a fair, honest read on that.

### Do I need online payment on my website?

Not at first, for most small shops. Show clearly which payment methods you accept, whether that's eSewa, Khalti, a bank QR or cash on delivery, and keep taking orders through WhatsApp or Viber. Add a proper online checkout once customers are asking to pay without talking to you.

### Can I build it myself with a free website builder?

You can, and for a start that is better than nothing. The usual problems come later. The site is slow on mobile data, the template looks like thousands of others, and it's hard to move off the builder once you outgrow it. If you go that way, own your domain from the start so you can move later without losing it.
